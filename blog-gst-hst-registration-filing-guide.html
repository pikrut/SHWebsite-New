GST/HST Registration and Filing Guide (2026)

GST is the federal goods and services tax; HST combines it with a provincial component in participating provinces. This guide explains registration, rates and filing for most businesses. Special rules apply to some activities and organizations.

Who Has to Register: The $30,000 Small-Supplier Threshold

For most businesses, the small-supplier test uses worldwide taxable supplies, including zero-rated supplies, from your businesses and applicable associates. It excludes specified items such as sales of capital property, financial services and goodwill from selling a business. The usual threshold is $30,000; some activities, including commercial ride-sharing, have different registration rules.

Single-quarter test: if you exceed $30,000 in one calendar quarter, charge GST/HST on the supply that takes you over the threshold. Register within 29 days of your effective registration date, which is no later than that supply.

Multiple-quarter test: if you exceed $30,000 over four or fewer consecutive quarters, but not in one quarter, small-supplier status ends at the end of the month following the quarter of exceedance. Registration takes effect no later than your first subsequent supply. Start charging from that date and register within 29 days.

Track calendar-quarter totals and consult CRA’s examples to identify the correct effective date.

Voluntary Registration: When It Pays to Register Early

Even if you're comfortably under $30,000, you're allowed to register voluntarily — and sometimes it's the smarter move. Once you're registered you can claim Input Tax Credits (ITCs) to recover the GST/HST you pay on business purchases, which is a real cash refund if your input tax exceeds the tax you collect. That's common in the early, investment-heavy stage of a business, or if most of your customers are themselves GST/HST-registered and don't care that you add tax to their invoice. The trade-off: you take on the paperwork of charging, filing, and remitting from day one. If you sell mainly to individual consumers, adding tax before you're required to can just make you more expensive than a competitor who hasn't registered yet.

How to Register: Getting Your Business Number and RT Account

Registration is free and, for most people, quick. The main route is CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO):

1. Get a Business Number (BN). If you don't already have one, BRO issues the nine-digit BN that identifies your business to the CRA. Sole proprietors register under their own name; incorporated businesses register under the corporation.

2. Add a GST/HST program account (the "RT" account). Your GST/HST account is a program account bolted onto the BN — it shows up as your BN followed by "RT0001." This is the account you'll use to file returns and remit tax.

3. Set your effective date and reporting period. Confirm when registration takes effect and which filing frequency applies. CRA ended phone-based BN and program-account registrations on November 3, 2025. Use Business Registration Online, or CRA’s alternative procedure if you cannot use it.

Once you're registered, start showing GST/HST on your invoices and include your GST/HST number so your customers can claim their own ITCs.

GST vs. HST Rates Across Canada in 2026

The applicable rate follows the place-of-supply rules: Ontario HST is 13%; Nova Scotia HST is 14% from April 1, 2025; New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island, and Newfoundland and Labrador HST is 15%. Elsewhere the federal GST is generally 5%, with separate provincial taxes where applicable. Zero-rated and exempt supplies have different treatment.

If you sell across provincial lines — which most remote and online businesses now do — the "place of supply" rules decide which province's rate applies, and it's usually driven by where your customer or the property is located. Get it wrong and you either over-charge a customer or under-remit to the CRA, so it's worth confirming the rate for each province you sell into.

Input Tax Credits (ITCs): Getting Your Money Back

ITCs are the reason registration isn't purely a cost. They let you recover the GST/HST you paid on purchases used in your commercial activities, netting it against the tax you collected. If you paid more input tax than you collected in a period, the difference comes back to you as a refund.

To claim an ITC you need proper documentation on file: the supplier's name, their GST/HST number, the date, the amount paid, and the tax charged. For larger purchases the CRA expects more detail, so keep the actual invoices, not just credit-card slips. Common ITC-eligible expenses include office supplies, equipment, software and professional services, the business portion of vehicle expenses, rent, and utilities.

Two limits catch people out. First, meals and entertainment: you can generally claim only 50% of the GST/HST on those costs, mirroring the 50% income-tax deduction limit. Second, you can only claim the business-use share of anything used partly for personal purposes. Keep the records — the ITC is only as good as the paper trail behind it.

Filing Frequencies and Due Dates: Annual, Quarterly, or Monthly

The CRA assigns your reporting period based on your annual taxable supplies. Businesses with $1.5 million or less can file annually; those over $1.5 million and up to $6 million file quarterly; and those over $6 million file monthly. You can always elect to file more often than required — many small businesses choose quarterly so ITC refunds come back sooner and the tax they've collected doesn't sit in the account for a full year.

Due dates depend on your period. For monthly and quarterly filers, both the return and the payment are due one month after the end of the reporting period. Annual filers generally have three months after their fiscal year-end to file and pay — but if you're an annual filer with a December 31 year-end and business income on your personal return, your return is due June 15 while any balance owing is still due April 30. Late filing and late payment both carry penalties and interest, so diarize the dates even in a quarter where you're claiming a refund.

Annual GST/HST filers may need quarterly instalments when the relevant net-tax conditions are met, generally involving $3,000 or more. Special calculations apply after a short first fiscal year. Check CRA’s instalment guidance rather than assuming the annual return means one annual payment.
